Customer Journey

CLARITY NOTES #002

The questions you keep answering are clues to your process

Repeated questions can reveal missing information, unclear handovers or instructions arriving too late. Use them to improve the process and customer journey behind your work.

Jayden • 5 min read • 11 August 2026

“What should I post today?”

It sounds like a content problem. Sometimes it is.

But if you keep answering the same questions in emails, calls and messages, there may be a more useful place to start: why do people need to keep asking?

Someone wondering how to book might need a clearer next step on your website. A new client asking where to upload documents might need better onboarding instructions. A team member checking which file is current might need one reliable place to find it.

A social post may help with awareness. It cannot reliably carry instructions that someone needs at a particular moment.

Repeated questions show you where to look

Questions are useful evidence about how people experience your business.

They can reveal:

  • missing information;
  • instructions that arrive too late;
  • competing versions of the same document;
  • an unclear handover;
  • a next step that depends on someone explaining it personally.

That does not mean every repeated question proves something is broken. People miss things, circumstances vary and some decisions genuinely need a conversation.

But if several people get stuck in the same place, it is worth examining the journey before writing another individual reply.

Your sent folder can be quite an informative process audit. Slightly less glamorous than a strategy workshop, but readily available.

Find the moment of uncertainty

Imagine a consultant receives the same question after every booking:

“What do you need from me before the session?”

They could publish a helpful post about preparing for a consultation.

The problem is that the next person who books may never see it.

A more dependable approach would put the instructions in the booking confirmation, link to one current preparation page and make any required action obvious.

Now look behind that message:

  • Does a confirmed booking reliably trigger the preparation step?
  • Who checks whether the information has arrived?
  • Where are the answers stored?
  • Can the consultant find them before the session?

The wording matters, but so does the process around it.

Match the information to the stage

People need different information at different points.

Before they enquire

Help them understand whether the service is relevant, how it works and what to do next.

A website page or useful article may be the right place.

While a decision is being made

Make scope, timing, responsibilities and outstanding questions easy to find.

That might belong in a proposal or a shared record, with a clear follow-up action.

After they commit

Explain what happens next, what you need from them and when they should expect to hear from you.

Booking confirmations, onboarding messages and client portals can support this.

During delivery

Keep progress, decisions and requests visible. Avoid making someone piece together a project from a long email chain.

At completion

Explain what has been handed over, where it lives and how to use or maintain it.

A completed project should not leave the customer wondering which link contains the final version.

Give each piece of information a home

You do not need to put everything in one application.

You do need to know which version is reliable and how people reach it when they need it.

A practical approach is to:

  1. Keep the core instructions in one maintained place.
  2. Link to them from the relevant message or workflow.
  3. Put the immediate next action directly in the message.
  4. Make someone responsible for keeping the instructions current.

Be thoughtful about access. A perfect set of instructions behind the wrong permissions is just another support request waiting to happen.

For repeatable internal work, a short checklist may be enough. For a client, a concise “what happens next” page might do the job better than an elaborate portal.

Keep useful conversations

Some questions deserve a personal answer.

A client asking how a recommendation applies to their circumstances needs judgement and context. A person who is uncertain about an important decision may need reassurance.

The aim is to remove avoidable confusion so those conversations get more attention.

Marketing still has a place. Customer questions can make excellent articles and posts. But first check whether the answer also needs to become part of the service, process or workspace.

Otherwise you can end up publishing helpful advice while the same confusion continues behind the scenes.

Your action

Choose one question you have answered more than once recently.

Write down:

  • who asks it;
  • when they need the answer;
  • where they currently look;
  • what should happen after they understand it.

Then improve that point in the journey. Put the answer where it is needed and give the next step a clear owner.
