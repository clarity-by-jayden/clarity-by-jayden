CLARITY NOTES #003

An enquiry came in. What happened next?

Enquiries, proposals and completed tasks only tell part of the story. Trace what happens between stages to find missing ownership, disconnected information and forgotten next actions.

Jayden • 4 min read • 11 August 2026

A post gets plenty of attention. An enquiry arrives. A proposal goes out.

Each looks like progress.

But what happened next?

Was the enquiry recorded? Did someone take responsibility for the reply? Did the proposal get a follow-up, or is it still sitting in a sent folder waiting for someone to remember?

It is possible to generate plenty of activity while losing track of the work that follows.

Look beyond the event

Most tools are good at showing what happened inside them.

Your social platform shows engagement. Your form tool shows submissions. Your email account shows messages sent. Your task list shows boxes ticked.

The harder question is how those events connect.

A form submission might be a new opportunity, an existing client's question or a supplier message. Until someone reviews it and decides the next action, “form received” tells you very little about progress.

The same applies to a proposal. Sending it completes one task. The wider piece of work remains open until someone knows the outcome or has decided when to follow up.

A completed task and a completed journey can be quite far apart.

Follow one journey across the gaps

Take a straightforward service enquiry:

  1. Someone submits a form.
  2. Their details reach the person responsible.
  3. The enquiry is reviewed.
  4. A reply or call is arranged.
  5. A proposal is prepared if appropriate.
  6. A decision is recorded.
  7. An agreed project moves into onboarding.

At each transition, ask:

  • What tells us this stage is complete?
  • Where is that information recorded?
  • Who owns the next action?
  • When does that action need to happen?
  • How would we notice if nothing happened?

These questions expose the gaps between systems.

A form can submit successfully while its notification lands in an unchecked inbox. A proposal can be opened without anyone recording the follow-up. A payment can arrive without the delivery process starting.

Everything may have worked technically. The person still ends up waiting.

Make the next action visible

You do not necessarily need a new platform.

For an open enquiry or project, a useful record usually needs enough information to answer:

  • What stage is this at?
  • Who is responsible?
  • What happens next?
  • When should it happen?
  • What are we waiting for?

Keep the detail proportionate. A small consultancy may manage this with a simple database and linked correspondence.

The important test is whether someone can understand the current position without searching several tools or asking the person who last touched it.

A “Waiting” status is more useful when it says what you are waiting for, who needs to respond and when you will check again.

Otherwise it can become a very polite way of losing something.

Measure the point you want to improve

Choose measures that help answer a practical question.

If enquiries are being missed, check whether each eligible enquiry reaches the shared record and has an owner.

If responses are slow, look at the time between receiving an enquiry and sending a meaningful reply.

If proposals drift, review overdue follow-ups and record outcomes, including decisions not to proceed.

If onboarding feels confusing, look for missing access, repeated questions and delays in completing the first step.

Define the stages consistently before comparing them. A booking, a qualified enquiry and a website visit represent different things.

And avoid assuming that one event caused another. A person may read several articles, receive a referral and contact you weeks later. Where the connection is uncertain, record what you know rather than inventing a tidy story.

Keep the reporting close to the work

A useful dashboard should help someone make a decision.

For example:

  • Which enquiries need attention today?
  • Which projects are waiting on information?
  • Which handovers have not been completed?
  • Where do people repeatedly get stuck?

Start with those questions, then decide what information is needed to answer them.

You may need clearer statuses or more consistent record-keeping before you need another chart.

Likes, views and traffic can still help you understand visibility. They are part of the picture. To understand how the business is working, you also need to follow the people and work that move beyond those numbers.

Your action

Choose one recent enquiry or project and trace it from the first contact to its current position.

Mark every point where you have to search, guess or ask someone what happened.

Pick one gap and give it a reliable record, a clear owner and a dated next action.

That is a useful place to start improving the system.
